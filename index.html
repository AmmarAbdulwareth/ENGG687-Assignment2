Why I Made This as a Game

The assignment said we could use any creative medium, and since I know how to code I decided to build an interactive game. I think ethics is one of those things where making the decision yourself teaches you more than reading about it. It is easy to say you would do the right thing in a hypothetical. It gets harder when there is a deadline, a supervisor telling you to let it slide, or operators who already found a workaround.

You play as a chemical engineer starting out in Alberta's energy sector, which is the career I am working toward. Each of the seven scenarios is an ethical dilemma drawn from our course lectures. You pick how to respond, the game explains why it matters, and you get a score at the end. I wrote every scenario from the perspective of someone who is going to face these situations for real.

How to play: You are starting your engineering career in Alberta. Each scenario is a year in your professional life. Your choices shape your reputation and determine where your career ends up. Pick the response that feels right. The game tracks your professional reputation and explains the ethical implications. No trick questions, but some answers are stronger than others.

My Reflection

When I enrolled in ENGG 687, I expected a course about the APEGA code and not cutting corners. Important, but straightforward. I was wrong. This course has changed the way I think about what being a professional engineer actually means.

The design justice lectures challenged me the most. I had not thought much about who gets a seat at the table when engineering decisions are made. In chemical engineering, our projects are refineries, pipelines, processing plants. They get built in specific places and affect specific communities. Costanza-Chock's framework [1] made me confront the fact that the communities most affected by industrial infrastructure are usually the ones with the least say. Environmental racism is a documented pattern, and as someone who wants to work in process engineering in Alberta, I will be making decisions that determine which communities bear those risks. I cannot unsee that now.

The neoliberalism material was uncomfortable in a different way. Randy Herrmann's line about engineers being "the foot soldiers of capitalism" stuck with me [3]. We build the systems. When market logic says cut the maintenance budget, the engineer has to decide whether to push back or go along. The pedestrian data drove it home: fatalities up 45% over a decade while cars got safer for drivers [4]. The costs of "efficiency" get pushed onto people with no power to fight back.

The safety lectures connect directly to my field. The Swiss cheese model [7] is something I want to carry for the rest of my career. Every removed layer of protection brings you closer to the day when everything lines up wrong. The IKEA Malm case [6] reminded me that "works as intended" is not enough. You have to design for how people actually behave. In chemical engineering, operators bypass interlocks and maintenance gets deferred. These are known behaviors. Designing systems that fail safely despite them is the job.

The algorithmic bias scenario was personal because I will almost certainly work with predictive tools in pipeline integrity. The objectivity lecture showed how facial recognition systems performed worse on underrepresented faces because of biased training data [1]. The same principle applies to pipeline inspection models: if historical data reflects decades of neglecting remote and Indigenous communities, an AI tool trained on that data will reproduce the neglect at scale. Catching that before deployment is not overstepping your role. It is the whole point of having an engineer in the room.

The alarm fatigue scenario connected the social media harm discussion [8] to something I will deal with in process plants. When a monitoring system sends 50 non-critical alerts per shift, operators stop responding to all of them, including the safety-critical ones. It is the same dynamic as social media platforms optimizing for engagement metrics even when they know the design causes harm. In process safety, alarm fatigue removes a Swiss cheese layer [7]. You are not designing for metrics. You are designing so that when a real alarm fires, someone actually responds.

The employment law scenario was the hardest to write because it involves a colleague, not a faceless corporation. But APEGA's obligations are personal [10]. Once you know about a safety concern, you cannot delegate your responsibility to someone else just because they saw the problem first. The Rivtow Marine case [9] established that knowledge of a defect creates liability. Loyalty to a colleague does not override duty to the public. That is a lesson I hope I never have to apply, but I know I need to be ready for it.

The tort law content grounded everything. Donoghue v Stevenson and Rivtow Marine [9] established that we owe a duty of care to people we may never meet. APEGA's disciplinary process [10] makes professional accountability non-optional. Getting my P.Eng. is still my goal, but this course has made me think harder about what that title demands. Not just whether the design meets code, but whether it actually serves the people it is supposed to serve.
